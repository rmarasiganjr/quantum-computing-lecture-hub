Shor's Algorithm

Factoring large numbers — the quantum algorithm that breaks the hard problem modern encryption relies on.

From an exponentially hard classical problem to a polynomial-time quantum one — by turning factoring into finding a repeating pattern.

The Problem

Factoring Large Numbers

Easy to Multiply, Hard to Un-Multiply

Multiplying two large primes together is fast. Given only the product, finding those two primes back is believed to be extremely hard — there's no known efficient classical shortcut.

Why This Matters

  • RSA encryption — used to secure web traffic, banking, email — relies entirely on factoring being hard
  • A public key is built from the product of two large primes
  • Breaking it means factoring that product back into its primes

Classical vs. Quantum

Exponential vs. Polynomial Time

The Best Classical Attack

The best known classical algorithm (the general number field sieve) still takes sub-exponential time — for the key sizes used today, that's effectively infeasible.

The Big Idea

Factoring Is Secretly Period-Finding

Shor's real insight: factoring N can be turned into finding the repeating pattern of a sequence — specifically, how often aˣ mod N cycles back to 1, for some number a you pick. Once you know that repeat length (the period), ordinary arithmetic usually hands you a factor.

Pick

Choose a number a smaller than N that shares no common factor with it.

Find the Period

A quantum computer finds how often aˣ mod N repeats — exponentially faster than checking by hand.

Extract a Factor

Plain classical arithmetic (gcd) turns that period into an actual factor of N — most of the time.

The Algorithm

Shor's Algorithm as a Pipeline

→
→
→

1 · Pick a — Classical

Choose a random a with 1 < a < N and gcd(a, N) = 1 (no shared factor with N). If a shares a factor with N by luck, that factor itself solves the problem immediately — this step can get lucky, but usually doesn't.

2 · Find the Period — Quantum

Build a superposition over every possible x, compute aˣ mod N in superposition, then use the Quantum Fourier Transform to read out the period r — the smallest r > 0 where aʳ mod N = 1. This is the one genuinely quantum step, and where the exponential speedup lives.

3 · Extract a Factor — Classical

If r is even, compute gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) and gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N) — one of these is often a real, non-trivial factor of N.

4 · Check It Worked — Classical

Sometimes r is odd, or a^(r/2) ≡ −1 (mod N) — both cases fail and hand back nothing useful. When that happens, just pick a new a and try again; success is likely within a few attempts.

Why It Matters

Where This Shows Up

Breaking RSA

RSA's security rests entirely on factoring being hard. A large enough, fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would factor RSA keys efficiently — breaking the scheme outright.

Post-Quantum Cryptography

This threat is why cryptographers are already standardizing new encryption schemes believed to resist quantum attacks — migrating critical systems well before large quantum computers arrive.

Read the Speedup Carefully

Why We Can't Break RSA Today

Qubits and Circuit Depth

Factoring a real 2,048-bit RSA key needs thousands of high-quality, error-corrected logical qubits and very deep circuits — far beyond what any quantum computer has today.

What's Actually Been Done

Real hardware demonstrations have factored small numbers like 15 and 21 — proving the algorithm works, not that today's hardware threatens real encryption.

Takeaway: Shor's algorithm is a proven exponential speedup in theory. The gap between "15 = 3 × 5 on a quantum computer" and "breaking real RSA keys" is still enormous — but it's the reason post-quantum cryptography work is happening now, not later.

Recap

The Whole Algorithm, in Four Ideas

  • Factoring N is turned into finding the period of aˣ mod N, for some chosen a
  • Finding that period is the one quantum step — done via the Quantum Fourier Transform, exponentially faster than any known classical method
  • Ordinary classical arithmetic (gcd) turns the period into an actual factor of N — most of the time
  • This is why RSA encryption is considered broken in principle by a large enough quantum computer — and why post-quantum cryptography exists
